Case Study · Cisco Internal App Marketplace

Scoping an AI Agent for a 230+ App Marketplace, Before a Line of Code Shipped

A 233-response survey that answered a scope question before it became a build mistake: which parts of an AI-powered discovery experience would employees actually trust, and which would quietly go unused?

Role: Lead UX Researcher Timeline: Apr 2026 · 3 weeks Method: Qualtrics Survey (n=233) Partners: Product, Design
🧭

The Problem

The marketplace team wanted an AI agent to help employees navigate 230+ internal apps, but had no evidence for where "helpful assistant" ended and "overstepping" began.

📊

My Role

I designed the survey, adapting a trust-tier framework validated weeks earlier in a parallel study, fielded it to active marketplace users, and delivered a scope-ready readout.

🔒

The Outcome

v1 scope was locked to transparent, explainable "inform" and "recommend" capabilities — deferring autonomous action to a later, trust-gated phase.

Background

An AI agent for 230+ apps needs a scope, not just a green light

The internal app marketplace had grown to 230+ tools, and an earlier research effort (my Apps Category Rationalization study) had already cleaned up how those apps were organized. The next question on the roadmap was an AI agent to help employees search, compare, and get recommendations — but "AI agent" meant something different to every stakeholder in the room: a chatbot to some, an autonomous provisioning system to others.

Rather than build a broad concept and test reactions to it, I had a faster option available: a three-tier trust framework I'd validated weeks earlier in the Approvals AI Agent research — informing, recommending, and acting — had already proven itself as a reliable way to predict where AI comfort would hold and where it would break down. This study existed to test whether that same pattern held for a completely different task (app discovery, not approvals), and if so, to use it to lock scope fast.

The risk wasn't building an AI agent that failed outright — it was building one so cautious it added no value, or so ambitious it got ignored. Scope had to be evidence-based on both ends.

Research Process

Three weeks, borrowing a validated framework instead of starting cold

Week 1
Design
Adapting a validated framework to a new domain
Rebuilt the inform/recommend/act comfort-scale approach from the Approvals AI Agent research around marketplace-specific tasks: search assistance, personalized recommendations, comparison summaries, and auto-request/install on the user's behalf.
Week 2
Field
Targeted distribution to active users
Distributed to employees who had browsed or installed an app in the past 90 days, rather than the full employee base — prioritizing signal from people who actually use the discovery experience today.
Week 3
Analyze & Decide
Analysis, readout, and scope lock
Ran quantitative and cross-tab analysis by role and tenure, coded open-ends for trust drivers, and delivered a readout that let product lock v1 scope in the same week — a notably fast research-to-decision cycle enabled by not having to derive a trust model from scratch.

Methodology

Reusing a validated model, testing it against a new task

I deliberately didn't treat this as a from-scratch discovery study. The Approvals AI Agent research had already shown that comfort with AI drops in a predictable step pattern as it moves from informing to acting — but that pattern had only been validated for one kind of task (approval decisions). This survey existed to test whether the same structure held for a very different kind of task: helping someone find and evaluate software.

Sample

  • Distributed to employees active in the marketplace within the past 90 days
  • 233 completes, segmented by role, tenure, and region for cross-tab analysis
  • Screened for at least one prior app search, comparison, or install

Instrument

  • Current discovery behavior and pain points
  • Capability comfort scale, reusing the inform / recommend / act tiering
  • Open-end: "What would make you trust an AI recommendation here?"
  • Interaction model preference: conversational assistant vs. inline suggestions vs. proactive notifications

Key Findings

The framework held — with one new twist

Finding 01

The trust ceiling for information is high; for action, it's near the floor

75% comfortable with AI for search/info retrieval — comfort with AI auto-requesting access on their behalf drops to ~14%

This confirmed the same step-down pattern seen in the Approvals research, in a completely different task domain — strong evidence this isn't an approvals-specific quirk, but a broader pattern in how employees relate to AI taking action on their behalf.

Finding 02

Recommendations were the most wanted feature — but trust was conditional, not automatic

46% ranked personalized recommendations as their single top-desired capability — the largest vote share of any option

Personalized recommendations beat both comparison tools and general search chat as the most-wanted capability. But open-ends made clear this wasn't unconditional enthusiasm: trust depended entirely on explainability. Employees wanted to see why an app was suggested, not just receive a suggestion.

"I would use it constantly if it just helped me find the right tool faster. I do not want it choosing for me."

Senior Engineer · AMER Region
Finding 03

Users want a navigator, not a gatekeeper

Open-end coding surfaced a consistent mental model: employees wanted AI to act as a fast, well-informed guide — never a decision-maker standing between them and the app catalog. This language closely echoed the "Investigator" persona from the Approvals research (wanting full context, distrusting anything that felt like hidden filtering), reinforcing that this trust pattern isn't specific to one team's tool — it appears to be a broader organizational expectation of AI.

Finding 04

Newer employees showed meaningfully higher comfort with higher-tier AI action

Cross-tab analysis by tenure found employees with less than a year at the company were notably more comfortable with higher-tier AI capabilities than tenured employees. This opened a concrete option product hadn't previously considered: a tenure-segmented pilot, rather than an all-or-nothing rollout.

The Framework, Applied

The same three tiers, a different domain

Reusing the trust-tier structure from the Approvals AI Agent research meant product didn't have to relitigate "should the AI just do it" from first principles — the tiers already carried organizational credibility.

Tier 1

Inform

75% comfortable

Conversational and inline search assistance, plain-language app descriptions, "what does this app actually do."

Build v1
Tier 2

Recommend

46% top choice

Personalized recommendations and comparison summaries — but only shipped with a visible, mandatory "why this" rationale attached.

Build v1, transparency-gated
Tier 3

Act

14% comfortable

Auto-requesting access or auto-installing apps on the user's behalf without an explicit action.

Held from v1

Recommendations

Locking v1 to what the data supported, not what was easiest to build

Domains where the trust-tier framework held

This research kept the AI agent's v1 scope locked to Tier 1 (informing) plus a transparency-gated Tier 2 (recommending) — preventing the team from over-investing in autonomous provisioning features the data clearly showed weren't trusted yet, while still shipping the single most-requested capability (personalized recommendations) rather than playing it too safe. Because the trust-tier language was already validated on a separate team's research, product and design moved from findings to a locked roadmap decision in the same week — a research-to-decision cycle considerably faster than the six weeks the original framework took to establish. As of this study, the AI agent had not yet shipped; the framework's real-world adoption numbers will be the subject of a post-launch follow-up.

Reflection

What I'd repeat, and what I'd change

What worked

  • Adapting an already-validated framework rather than building from scratch let the team move from research to a locked scope decision in about a third of the time the original study took
  • Segmenting the sample to active marketplace users only (not the full employee base) meant every response came from someone with real, recent context for the questions being asked
  • Having a second, independent domain confirm the same trust-tier pattern gave leadership meaningfully more confidence than a single study could have on its own

What I'd do differently

  • I'd pair the survey with a small number of quick unmoderated concept tests of the "why this recommendation" explainability UI — the open-ends made clear explainability is the trust hinge, but a survey alone can't tell you which specific explanation format (source-based, peer-based, role-based) will actually land
  • I'd build the tenure-segmented pilot design into this study's scope from the start, rather than flagging it as a "next" recommendation for a future study to pick up
