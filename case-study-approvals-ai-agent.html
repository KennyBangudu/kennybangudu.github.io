Case Study · Enterprise Approvals Platform

Defining Trust Boundaries for an Enterprise Approvals AI Agent

A two-phase research program — a 405-response global survey followed by 11 scenario-based interviews — that answered the question leadership hadn't yet asked precisely enough: not "should we build an AI agent," but "what, specifically, will approvers actually trust it to do?"

Role: Lead UX Researcher, Phase I & II Timeline: Mar – Apr 2026 · 6 weeks Methods: Survey (n=405) + IDIs (n=11) Partners: Product, Design, Engineering
🎯

The Problem

Leadership wanted to "build an AI agent" for approvals with no evidence-based definition of what that meant. Left unresearched, the team risked building autonomous features nobody trusted, or under-building assistance that left the real friction untouched.

🔬

My Role

I designed and led both phases solo — instrument design, sampling, moderation, thematic synthesis, and the executive readout — building the shared research foundation the org still references today.

🚀

The Outcome

A three-tier trust framework, two approver mental models, and an automation threshold roadmap — adopted as shared strategic language for enterprise AI product direction beyond just this one team.

Background

"Build an AI agent" is not a research question

Approvers across the enterprise approvals platform were navigating a familiar frustration: requests arrived stripped of context, delegation during time off relied on informal workarounds, and volume was climbing as the org scaled. Leadership's instinct was to point at generative AI as the fix — but "build an AI agent" is a mandate, not a scope. It says nothing about which tasks approvers would actually hand to AI, which they'd want AI to merely inform, and which they'd never delegate at all.

My job was to convert that ambition into a testable research question before a single line of product spec got written: where, precisely, is the trust boundary — and what does the AI need to do on either side of it to earn adoption instead of resistance?

"If we build the wrong tier first, we don't just waste a quarter — we teach 6,800 approvers that the AI agent isn't for them. Trust is the resource we can't get back once we burn it."

That risk is why this was scoped as a two-phase program rather than a single survey. A survey alone would tell us what approvers said they wanted; it wouldn't tell us why, under what conditions that preference held, or where self-reported comfort would break down against a real scenario. Phase II existed specifically to pressure-test Phase I before the roadmap was locked.

Research Process

Six weeks, two phases, one continuous thread

Rather than running the survey and interviews as separate, disconnected engagements, I designed Phase II to directly interrogate what Phase I surfaced — using real quantitative signal to sharpen which scenarios were worth probing qualitatively.

Methodology

Two instruments, built to interrogate each other

Each phase was designed with a specific methodological weakness of the other in mind. The survey could tell us the shape of comfort across a large, statistically representative sample — but self-reported comfort scales are notoriously optimistic compared to real behavior. The interviews used scenario-based probes rather than direct questions specifically to counter that gap: instead of asking "would you trust AI to flag anomalies," I walked each participant through a live exception case and watched what they actually wanted to see before acting.

Phase I — Survey

  • Distributed via Qualtrics to 6,815 employees with approval authority
  • 12-item capability comfort scale spanning inform → recommend → act
  • Forced-rank, point-allocation channel preference exercise (not a simple pick-one, to surface real relative strength of preference)
  • Open-ended: "What would make you trust an AI recommendation on this?"
  • 405 completes · 6% response rate · 96% completion among starters

Phase II — In-Depth Interviews

  • 11 participants screened for a mix of approval volume, seniority band, and region
  • Three scenario-based probes: routine approval, ambiguous exception, delegated/vacation case
  • Semi-structured follow-up on delegation habits and current workarounds
  • Thematic analysis cross-referenced line-by-line against Phase I survey findings
  • 60-minute video sessions across AMER, EMEAR, APJC

Every Phase II finding was checked against Phase I data before being written up. Nowhere did the interviews contradict the survey — instead, they explained the conditions under which each survey preference held, which is exactly the gap a single-method study would have left open.

Key Findings

Five patterns that reshaped the brief

Finding 01

The problem was context, not volume

Only 22% cited approval volume as their top pain point — 68% cited insufficient context to decide confidently

Leadership's working assumption was that an AI agent should primarily reduce workload. The data said otherwise: approvers weren't overwhelmed by how many requests arrived, they were under-informed about each one. Requests showed up without policy context, requester history, or an explanation of why something needed a second look.

"If it can tell me why something needs my approval and flag anything unusual, I would use it every single day. Right now I am flying blind."

Senior Manager · APJC Region · Phase I open-end
Finding 02

Trust follows a strict hierarchy — inform, then recommend, then act

Comfort dropped from 78% (AI informs) to 41% (AI recommends) to 12% (AI acts autonomously)

Across every capability tested, comfort fell in a consistent, predictable step-pattern as AI moved from surfacing information toward taking independent action. This wasn't noise — it held across region, seniority, and approval volume, and it became the backbone of the trust framework below.

Finding 03

Channel mattered as much as capability

The team's internal messaging tool scored 762 points in forced-rank preference — 200+ points ahead of the next option

Approvers didn't want another destination to check — they wanted approvals to arrive inside the tool they already lived in daily. A portal-first digest, even a well-designed one, ranked a distant second. This single finding redirected the entire delivery model for v1 toward a push-based, in-channel experience.

Finding 04

Delegation is a silent failure point, held together by manual workarounds

11 of 11 Phase II participants described delegation as entirely manual today

Every single interview participant, without exception, described managing delegation through spreadsheets, verbal handoffs, or simply letting requests pile up during time off. There was no structured system support at all — an issue the survey hadn't been designed to surface, and one that became a top mid-term roadmap priority once the interviews exposed its severity.

"I do not need the AI to decide for me. I need it to show up with everything I need to decide quickly, then get out of the way."

Director-level Approver · AMER Region · Phase II interview
Finding 05

Two distinct mental models — not one "average approver"

The interviews surfaced two clearly different approaches to the work, each with different needs from an AI agent. Designing a single interaction model for both would have satisfied neither.

The Triager

Pattern: High volume, low individual stakes per request. Wants speed above all.

Wants from AI: Pre-filtering, batching, bulk-clear for routine categories. Trusts AI to pre-sort, doesn't need deep rationale on low-stakes items.

The Investigator

Pattern: Lower volume, higher stakes per request. Wants depth before speed.

Wants from AI: Full context compiled and surfaced up front. Actively distrusts pre-filtering — wants to see everything, then decide, not have anything hidden by an algorithm.

The Synthesis

A three-tier trust framework, built from the data

The two-phase data converged on a clean structure: comfort with AI fell into three distinct tiers, each requiring a different level of earned trust before it could ship. This framework became the shared vocabulary product and design used to resolve scope debates for the rest of the program.

Tier 1

Knowledge Orchestrator

78% comfortable

AI surfaces policy context, requester history, and precedent, and flags anomalies in plain language. Never decides — only informs.

Build now
Tier 2

Recommender

41% comfortable

AI proposes a decision with visible rationale; a human confirms. Comfort is real but conditional on Tier 1 trust being established first.

Opt-in, v2
Tier 3

Autonomous Actor

12% comfortable

AI approves or denies within defined thresholds without review. Only viable for narrowly scoped, low-stakes, fully reversible decisions.

Future, gated

Recommendations

An automation threshold roadmap, not a feature list

Rather than hand product a flat backlog, I sequenced recommendations against the trust tiers directly — so each release earns the right to ship the next tier, instead of front-loading autonomy the data said nobody wanted yet.

Now · Tier 1
Unified, enriched approval digest delivered via the internal messaging channel, not a portal
Inline policy and precedent links on every approval card
Plain-language anomaly flags — flag and explain, never auto-block
Next · Tier 1–2
Structured, auditable AI-assisted delegation, replacing spreadsheet workarounds entirely
Opt-in "Recommended Decision" mode for select low-stakes approval types, with visible rationale always shown
Persona-aware default views: batch mode for Triagers, full-context mode for Investigators
Later · Tier 2–3
Bulk-approval clustering for high-volume Triager workflows
Opt-in Tier 3 auto-approval, restricted to narrowly scoped, fully reversible, low-risk categories only
Recurring trust-tracking pulse survey to gate the timing of any further Tier 3 expansion

Contradictions between Phase I and Phase II findings

The trust framework, approver mental models, and automation threshold roadmap were adopted as shared strategic language well beyond the Approvals team — the same tiering logic was reused directly by the App Marketplace AI Agent research just weeks later, letting that team skip re-deriving a framework from scratch. Tier 1 recommendations shipped first into the messaging bot channel expansion; the following quarter's Approvals Tool Health Report showed the bot channel handling 6,731 actions in its first month at a 98.8% approval rate — an early signal that leading with Tier 1 trust-building, rather than jumping straight to autonomy, was the right call.

Reflection

What I'd repeat, and what I'd change

What worked

  • Designing Phase II specifically to interrogate Phase I's ambiguous zones, rather than running two independent studies, meant every qualitative finding either reinforced or sharpened a quantitative one — never contradicted it
  • Scenario-based probes surfaced behavior self-report couldn't — the delegation finding only emerged because participants had to walk through a real vacation scenario, not because I asked "how do you handle delegation" directly
  • Framing the deliverable as a tiered roadmap, not a findings deck, gave product a decision-ready artifact instead of one more thing to interpret

What I'd do differently

  • A three-week gap between Phase I close and Phase II kickoff let org priorities drift slightly mid-study; running the phases with tighter overlap would reduce that risk
  • I'd formalize the tier framework as a reusable rubric even earlier — by the time the App Marketplace team needed it, I was recreating documentation from memory instead of handing off a ready reference
  • I'd add a lightweight quantitative check-in a few weeks post-launch to see whether real behavior matched stated Tier 1 comfort, rather than waiting for the next full research cycle to find out
